ER diagrams, cardinality & weak entities 6
DB-U1-Q01MCQeasy

In an entity–relationship diagram, a weak entity set is depicted by

  1. a single rectangle.
  2. a double rectangle.
  3. a single diamond.
  4. a double diamond.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. ER notation. A weak entity set has no key attribute of its own; its existence depends on an owner entity set, so it is drawn as a double rectangle. Its identifying relationship is drawn as a double diamond, its partial key is underlined with a dashed line, and its total participation in the identifying relationship is shown by a double line.

Step by step.

  1. A weak entity set cannot be identified by its own attributes, only by (owner key + partial key).
  2. The standard Chen notation distinguishes it with the double outline: double rectangle for the set, double diamond for the identifying relationship.
  3. Hence the correct symbol for the weak entity set itself is the double rectangle.

Answer: Option (B) — a double rectangle.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) A single rectangle denotes a strong (regular) entity set.
  • (C) A diamond denotes a relationship set, not an entity set.
  • (D) A double diamond denotes the identifying relationship of a weak entity set.

Common misconception. Confusing the double rectangle (weak entity set) with the double diamond (identifying relationship). They are different symbols for different things and a question may ask for either.

Faster method. If the option mentions a diamond at all, it is describing a relationship, not an entity set.

Takeaway. Fix the four ER symbols once: rectangle = entity set, double rectangle = weak entity set, diamond = relationship, double diamond = identifying relationship.

DB-U1-Q02MCQmedium

Entity set A has a many-to-one relationship R with entity set B. The participation of A in R is total and the participation of B in R is partial. Which one of the following is necessarily TRUE?

  1. Every entity of B is related to at least one entity of A.
  2. Every entity of A is related to exactly one entity of B.
  3. Every entity of B is related to exactly one entity of A.
  4. R must be merged into B because B's participation is partial.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Cardinality ratio plus participation constraint. “Many-to-one from A to B” means each entity of A is related to at most one entity of B, while one entity of B may be related to many entities of A. Total participation of A adds “at least one”, and the two bounds together give “exactly one”.

Step by step.

  1. Many-to-one A → B: an A-entity can be linked to at most one B-entity.
  2. Total participation of A: every A-entity is linked to at least one B-entity.
  3. At most one and at least one give exactly one B-entity for every A-entity.
  4. Partial participation of B only says some B-entities may have no A-entity at all; it constrains nothing about A.

Answer: Option (B) — every entity of A is related to exactly one entity of B.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) B's participation is partial, so a B-entity need not appear in R at all.
  • (C) Many-to-one allows many A-entities to share one B-entity, so B is not related to a unique A.
  • (D) A relationship is merged into an entity set only for a one-to-one or one-to-many case without loss, and partial participation of B does not force it.

Common misconception. Students read “partial participation of B” as if it weakened the constraint on A. Participation constrains only the entity set it is attached to.

Faster method. Total participation on the “many” side of a many-to-one turns “at most one” into “exactly one”.

Takeaway. Separate the two facts: the cardinality ratio bounds the count from above, participation bounds it from below. Combine them for the answer.

DB-U1-Q03MSQeasy

In a Chen-notation ER diagram, which of the following are drawn with a double outline or a double diamond? (Select all that apply.)

  1. A weak entity set.
  2. The identifying relationship of a weak entity set.
  3. A multivalued attribute.
  4. A derived attribute.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. ER notation distinguishes four special constructs. Double rectangle = weak entity set, double diamond = identifying relationship, double ellipse = multivalued attribute, dashed ellipse = derived attribute.

Step by step.

  1. (A) A weak entity set is drawn as a double rectangle — true.
  2. (B) The identifying relationship is drawn as a double diamond — true.
  3. (C) A multivalued attribute uses a double ellipse, not a double outline on a set or a relationship.
  4. (D) A derived attribute uses a dashed ellipse.

Answer: Options (A) and (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) uses a double ellipse, which is a different shape.
  • (D) uses a dashed ellipse.

Common misconception. The double ellipse for a multivalued attribute is confused with the double rectangle for a weak entity set. Both are “double”, but the shape differs and so does the meaning.

Takeaway. Remember the pairing shape-to-meaning: double rectangle / double diamond belong together as the weak-entity pair; double ellipse is an attribute-level symbol.

DB-U1-Q04NATmedium

Entity set E owns weak entity set W through an identifying relationship R. E has 4 entities, R permits at most 3 W-entities per E-entity, and every E-entity participates totally in R. What is the maximum possible number of entities in W?
(Enter an integer.)

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A weak entity set's cardinality is bounded by the owner's cardinality times the per-owner limit. Total participation of the owner gives a lower bound, the per-owner limit gives the upper bound.

Step by step.

  1. Every E-entity must own at least one W-entity (total participation), so |W| ≥ 4.
  2. Each E-entity may own at most 3 W-entities, so |W| ≤ 4 × 3 = 12.
  3. The upper bound 12 is attained when all 4 E-entities own the full 3 W-entities each.
  4. So the maximum is 12.

Answer: 12.

Common misconception. Adding the two numbers instead of multiplying, or forgetting that a weak entity is owned by exactly one owner — which is what makes the count a product rather than a sum.

Takeaway. For an identifying relationship the owner side is one-to-many, so multiply the owner count by the per-owner limit; the owner's participation decides the minimum, the limit decides the maximum.

DB-U1-Q05MCQhard

In ER modelling, aggregation is used to

  1. combine several attributes of one entity set into a single composite attribute.
  2. treat a relationship set together with its participating entity sets as one higher-level entity set that can then take part in another relationship.
  3. replace a weak entity set by a strong entity set.
  4. convert a ternary relationship into three binary relationships.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Aggregation is the ER construct that removes a limitation of plain ER diagrams: a relationship set cannot participate in another relationship. Aggregating a relationship plus its entity sets yields a single abstract entity that can.

Step by step.

  1. Plain ER forbids a relationship set from being an endpoint of another relationship.
  2. Example: a project is done by employees using machines. “Uses” relates the (employee, project) pair to a machine, not an employee to a machine directly.
  3. Aggregation groups Employee–Works_On–Project into one entity set and lets that set participate in Uses.
  4. So aggregation abstracts a relationship together with its participants into a higher-level entity set.

Answer: Option (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) describes a composite attribute, drawn as an ellipse with sub-ellipses.
  • (C) is not an ER operation at all; a weak entity set stays weak unless the schema is redesigned.
  • (D) is a modelling decision about arity, not aggregation.

Common misconception. Aggregation is confused with generalization (which factors common attributes of entity sets into a superclass) and with the composite-attribute notation. All three “combine” things, but at different levels.

Faster method. If the sentence mentions “a relationship participating in another relationship”, the answer is aggregation.

Takeaway. Aggregation = relationship-as-entity. Generalization = entity-as-subclass. Composite attribute = attribute-of-attribute.

DB-U1-Q06MCQeasy

A multivalued attribute in an ER diagram is represented by

  1. a single ellipse.
  2. a double ellipse.
  3. a dashed ellipse.
  4. a rectangle.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Attribute notation in Chen's model. A multivalued attribute can hold a set of values for one entity, and is drawn as a double ellipse. A derived attribute is drawn with a dashed ellipse; a key attribute has its name underlined.

Step by step.

  1. Multivalued means several values per entity, e.g. phone numbers of a person.
  2. The extra value needs an extra outline, so the symbol is the double ellipse.
  3. A derived attribute (computed from others, e.g. age from date of birth) uses a dashed ellipse instead.

Answer: Option (B) — a double ellipse.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) A single ellipse is a simple, single-valued attribute.
  • (C) A dashed ellipse is a derived attribute.
  • (D) A rectangle is an entity set.

Common misconception. The double ellipse is confused with the double rectangle of a weak entity set. Note that the doubling pattern is applied to the base shape: rectangle for sets, ellipse for attributes.

Takeaway. Learn the four attribute shapes together: single ellipse (simple), double ellipse (multivalued), dashed ellipse (derived), underlined name (key).

Relational model & constraints 6
DB-U1-Q07MCQeasy

Relation R(A, B, C) has primary key (A, B) and a foreign key C that references S(C). Which one of the following is TRUE?

  1. A and B cannot be NULL, but C may be NULL.
  2. C cannot be NULL, but A may be NULL.
  3. Any of A, B, C may be NULL.
  4. A alone must be unique.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Entity integrity says no attribute of a primary key may be NULL; referential integrity says a foreign key must either be NULL or match an existing referenced value. The two rules apply to different attributes here, so both can hold at once.

Step by step.

  1. Entity integrity: every attribute of the primary key (A, B) is NOT NULL. So A and B cannot be NULL.
  2. Referential integrity constrains C: each C-value must be NULL or equal some S.C value.
  3. Nothing forbids C from being NULL, because C is not part of R's primary key.
  4. Hence (A, B) are mandatory, C is optional.

Answer: Option (A).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (B) reverses the two rules.
  • (C) contradicts entity integrity.
  • (D) A is only part of the key; A alone may repeat.

Common misconception. Students assume a foreign key can never be NULL. It can, provided the FK attributes are not part of the primary key — that is exactly how “optional” relationships are modelled.

Takeaway. Check whether the FK overlaps the primary key. No overlap → NULL is allowed. Overlap → entity integrity forbids NULL.

DB-U1-Q08MCQmedium

Let R(A, B, C, D) satisfy the functional dependencies {AB → C, C → D, D → A}. How many candidate keys does R have?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A candidate key is a minimal set of attributes whose closure is the whole relation. Compute closures of each attribute alone, then of the promising pairs.

Step by step.

  1. A⁺ = A; B⁺ = B; C⁺ = C D A = ACD (using C → D and D → A); D⁺ = D A = AD. None is the whole relation.
  2. AB⁺ = A B C D = ABCD. AB is a key, and neither A nor B alone is, so AB is a candidate key.
  3. BC⁺ = B C D A = ABCD. BC is minimal (B⁺, C⁺ both fail), so BC is a candidate key.
  4. BD⁺ = B D A = ABD, and AB → C applies, so BD⁺ = ABCD. BD is minimal, so BD is a candidate key.
  5. AC⁺ = ACD, AD⁺ = AD, CD⁺ = ACD — none is the whole relation. The answer is 3.

Answer: Option (C) — 3 candidate keys (AB, BC, BD).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) misses BC and BD.
  • (B) misses BD.
  • (D) over-counts; AC, AD and CD are not keys.

Common misconception. Stopping at AB, or counting AB and BA as different keys. Also easy to miss BD, which needs the two-step closure D → A followed by AB → C.

Faster method. Here C and D never appear in a closure that contains B, so B is in every key. That forces you to test AB, BC and BD — exactly three.

Takeaway. Write every single-attribute closure first. Any attribute absent from all of them must appear in every candidate key; then test the minimal pairs containing it.

DB-U1-Q09MSQmedium

Which of the following statements about keys of a relation are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. Every relation has at least one candidate key.
  2. A superkey that has no proper subset which is also a superkey is a candidate key.
  3. A relation can have at most one candidate key.
  4. A primary key attribute may contain NULL values.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Keys are defined through functional determination on the whole set of attributes. The set of all attributes of a relation is always a superkey, so minimal superkeys always exist.

Step by step.

  1. The full attribute set functionally determines itself, so it is a superkey; shrinking it until it is minimal yields a candidate key. Hence at least one candidate key always exists.
  2. A candidate key is precisely a minimal superkey — that is the definition given in (B).
  3. A relation may have several candidate keys; one of them is chosen as the primary key.
  4. Entity integrity forbids NULL in any primary key attribute.

Answer: Options (A) and (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) is false — a relation may have several candidate keys, and exactly one is picked as primary.
  • (D) is false — entity integrity requires primary key attributes to be NOT NULL.

Common misconception. The word “candidate” is read as “optional”, suggesting there might be none. Every relation has at least one; what varies is how many.

Takeaway. Two facts to keep: the full attribute set is always a superkey (so keys exist), and minimality is what separates candidate keys from superkeys.

DB-U1-Q10NATeasy

Relation R(A, B, C) satisfies the functional dependency A → B and no other non-trivial dependency. How many superkeys does R have?
(Enter an integer.)

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A superkey is any attribute set whose closure is all of R. With only A → B, the closure of a set S is S ∪ {B} if A ∈ S, and S otherwise; a superkey must therefore contain A and C.

Step by step.

  1. The candidate key: A⁺ = AB, so A alone is not a key; AC⁺ = ABC. Hence AC is the only candidate key.
  2. Every superkey must contain the candidate key AC.
  3. The only attributes available to add are the remaining ones, here just B, giving the supersets AC and ABC.
  4. So R has exactly 2 superkeys.

Answer: 2.

Common misconception. Counting only AC and forgetting that a superkey may contain extra attributes, or counting A and C separately as keys.

Takeaway. Find the candidate key first; every superkey is then a superset of it, so the count is 2 raised to the number of leftover attributes.

DB-U1-Q11MCQhard

Let R(A, B, C, D, E) satisfy the functional dependencies {A → BC, CD → E, B → D, E → A}. How many candidate keys does R have?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 5
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Candidate keys are minimal sets with full closure. Because the dependencies form a cycle (A → B → D → E → A together with C on one edge), the relation has several minimal keys and every attribute belongs to at least one of them.

Step by step.

  1. A⁺: A → BC gives B, C; B → D gives D; CD → E gives E. So A⁺ = ABCDE. A is a candidate key.
  2. E⁺: E → A, then A → BC, then B → D, then CD → E. So E⁺ = ABCDE. E is a candidate key.
  3. BC⁺: B → D, CD → E, E → A, A → BC. So BC⁺ = ABCDE, and B⁺ = BD, C⁺ = C — BC is minimal.
  4. CD⁺: CD → E, E → A, A → BC, B → D. So CD⁺ = ABCDE, and C⁺ = C, D⁺ = D — CD is minimal.
  5. B⁺ = BD, C⁺ = C, D⁺ = D, BD⁺ = BD — none is the whole relation, and every other set contains one of A, E, BC, CD. So there are exactly 4 candidate keys.

Answer: Option (C) — 4 candidate keys (A, E, BC, CD).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) misses BC and CD.
  • (B) misses one of E, BC, CD.
  • (D) over-counts: BD⁺ = BD is not a key.

Common misconception. Missing E or CD because the cycle makes closure look “too easy”. The dependency cycle A → B → D → E → A means closure from any member reaches all the others, so several attributes become keys in their own right.

Faster method. Any attribute whose single-attribute closure is the whole relation is a key by itself; here that is true of A and E, and the C-bearing pairs add two more.

Takeaway. When FDs form a cycle, compute closure from each single attribute first — every attribute that closes to ABCDE is already a key and needs no partner.

DB-U1-Q12MCQeasy

Relation R(A, B) has A as its primary key and B as an ordinary nullable attribute. Which one of the following tuples, if inserted into R, violates a constraint implied by this schema?

  1. (NULL, 5)
  2. (1, NULL)
  3. (1, 5)
  4. (2, NULL)
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Entity integrity: no component of a primary key may be NULL. B is not part of the key, so NULL is allowed there and only uniqueness would ever reject it — and NULLs do not clash with each other under this rule.

Step by step.

  1. A is the primary key, so every tuple must supply a non-NULL value for A.
  2. (NULL, 5) supplies no key value, so it violates entity integrity.
  3. (1, NULL) and (2, NULL) are fine: the key values 1 and 2 are present, B is nullable.
  4. (1, 5) is fine as long as 1 is not already present.

Answer: Option (A) — (NULL, 5).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (B) and (D) have a valid key value and a NULL in a nullable column.
  • (C) has a valid key value and no NULL.

Common misconception. Students reject (1, NULL) thinking no attribute may be NULL. Nullability is a schema property; only primary-key attributes are forced to be non-NULL.

Takeaway. Look at where NULL sits. Inside the primary key → rejected. Outside it → allowed unless the column is declared NOT NULL.

Relational algebra: σ, π, join, division 8
DB-U1-Q13MCQeasy

In relational algebra, the division operator R ÷ S is used to express

  1. universal quantification — the “for all” condition.
  2. aggregation of a numeric attribute over groups.
  3. an outer join that preserves unmatched tuples.
  4. elimination of duplicates from a bag.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Division is the relational-algebra counterpart of universal quantification. R ÷ S keeps those A-values of R(A, B) that are paired in R with every B-value appearing in S.

Step by step.

  1. Write R(A, B) and S(B). R ÷ S = { a | for every b ∈ S, (a, b) ∈ R }.
  2. The “for every b ∈ S” is exactly a ∀ condition, so division expresses queries such as “students who took all courses”.
  3. It is not primitive: R ÷ S = π_A(R) − π_A( (π_A(R) × S) − R ).

Answer: Option (A) — universal quantification.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (B) Aggregation is not an operator of the basic algebra at all; it is added as γ in extended algebra.
  • (C) Outer joins are extensions of ⋈, written ⟕ / ⟖ / ⟗.
  • (D) Duplicate elimination is implicit in every algebra operator, not the job of ÷.

Common misconception. Division is mistaken for ordinary arithmetic division or for a join. It answers “for all”, not “how many”.

Takeaway. Read division as a nested quantifier: keep a only if nothing in S is missing from a's row.

DB-U1-Q14MCQmedium

Let R(A, B) = {(1, 10), (2, 20), (3, 10)} and S(B, C) = {(10, p), (20, q), (30, r)}. How many tuples are there in R ⋈ S (natural join)?

  1. 2
  2. 3
  3. 4
  4. 5
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A natural join equates the common attribute B and concatenates matching pairs. Its size is Σ_b r_b · s_b over the common values, where r_b and s_b are the multiplicities of b in R and S.

Step by step.

  1. Common attribute is B.
  2. B = 10 appears once in R and once in S → 1 × 1 = 1 tuple: (1, 10, p).
  3. B = 20 appears once in R and once in S → 1 tuple: (2, 20, q).
  4. B = 30 appears only in S → contributes nothing; B = 10 also occurs twice in R, so (3, 10, p) is a second tuple for B = 10.
  5. Total = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 tuples.

Answer: Option (B) — 3 tuples.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) counts distinct B values instead of tuples.
  • (C) and (D) invent matches for B = 30, which is absent from R.

Common misconception. Students assume one output tuple per distinct value of B (giving 2), forgetting that repeated B values in R each match. The join is a cross product restricted to equal B.

Takeaway. Count multiplicities: for each common value b, multiply its count in R by its count in S and sum. Here 1·1 + 1·1 = 2 for B = 10 and B = 20, plus the second B = 10 row, giving 3.

DB-U1-Q15MSQeasy

Which of the following statements about relational algebra operators are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. Selection σ filters tuples and projection π filters attributes.
  2. Projection π removes duplicate tuples from its result.
  3. R − S and S − R are always equal.
  4. R ∪ S is defined only when R and S are union-compatible.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Relational algebra is set-based, so every operator produces a relation, i.e. a set with no duplicate tuples, and the binary set operators require matching schemas.

Step by step.

  1. (A) σ keeps rows satisfying a predicate, π keeps a chosen list of columns — true.
  2. (B) Because the result is a relation (a set), π collapses duplicates — true.
  3. (C) Set difference is not symmetric: R − S keeps R's tuples absent from S, which is a different set from S − R.
  4. (D) Union requires the same number of attributes with compatible domains in the same order — true.

Answer: Options (A), (B) and (D).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) fails whenever the two relations are not identical: {1} − {2} = {1} but {2} − {1} = {2}.

Common misconception. The duplicate elimination of π is the usual surprise: SQL's SELECT does not remove duplicates, but relational algebra's π does. Mixing the two semantics is a standard error.

Takeaway. Remember: algebra = sets, SQL = bags. Every algebra operator, including π, returns a set.

DB-U1-Q16NATmedium

Let R(A, B) = {(a₁, b₁), (a₁, b₂), (a₂, b₁), (a₃, b₁), (a₃, b₂), (a₄, b₃)} and S(B) = {b₁, b₂}. How many tuples does R ÷ S contain?
(Enter an integer.)

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. R ÷ S keeps an A-value only if it is paired with every B-value of S in R. Check each A-value against the full set {b₁, b₂}.

Step by step.

  1. a₁ is paired with b₁ and with b₂ → qualifies.
  2. a₂ is paired only with b₁ → does not qualify.
  3. a₃ is paired with b₁ and with b₂ → qualifies.
  4. a₄ is paired only with b₃, which is not even in S → does not qualify.
  5. So R ÷ S = {a₁, a₃}, two tuples.

Answer: 2.

Common misconception. Requiring only “at least one” match, which would keep a₂ and a₄ as well. Division demands every value of S, not any one of them.

Takeaway. Group R by A and check set-equality against S. Here a₁ and a₃ both map to exactly {b₁, b₂}.

DB-U1-Q17MCQhard

Consider the schema Doctor(did, dname, dage) and Patient(pid, rdid, pname, page), where rdid is a foreign key referencing did. Assume every doctor has at least one patient. Consider the relational algebra query
π_did(Doctor) − π_did( Doctor ⋈(did = rdid) ∧ (dage ≤ page) Patient )
The query evaluates to the set of dids of doctors whose age is greater than that of

  1. some patient.
  2. all patients.
  3. some of his/her patients.
  4. all of his/her patients.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Read the query as a set difference: start from all doctors, then remove the doctors that have a patient whose age is at least the doctor's. What survives is exactly the doctors for whom no such patient exists.

Step by step.

  1. Doctor ⋈(did = rdid) ∧ (dage ≤ page) Patient keeps pairs where the doctor's age is ≤ some patient's age, i.e. the doctor is not older than that patient.
  2. Projecting on did gives the doctors having at least one patient who is at least as old as they are.
  3. Subtracting this from π_did(Doctor) leaves the doctors who have no such patient.
  4. “No patient is at least as old as the doctor” means the doctor is strictly older than every patient — all of his/her patients.
  5. The assumption that every doctor has at least one patient matters: without it, a doctor with no patients would also survive, and the query would not mean “older than all”.

Answer: Option (D) — all of his/her patients.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) and (C) describe the set that is removed, not the set that survives.
  • (B) would require a ∀ condition expressed with division, not a single join.

Common misconception. Answering “some patient” by stopping at the join instead of completing the difference, or reading the join's ≤ as if it removed patients rather than doctors.

Faster method. Any “−” over a join that encodes “exists a violating tuple” is the standard pattern for a universal statement: removing the violators leaves the ones that satisfy “for all”.

Takeaway. Translate each operator in turn: ⋈ with a condition = “there exists”, π = “the did of”, − = “but not”. Composing them gives “doctors for whom there is no patient at least as old”, which is “older than all”.

DB-U1-Q18MCQeasy

The relational algebra operator that returns a relation containing only a chosen subset of the attributes of its input is

  1. σ (select)
  2. π (projection)
  3. ρ (rename)
  4. ⋈ (join)
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. The six primitive operators act on different aspects of a relation: σ on rows, π on columns, ρ on names, and ×, ∪, − on whole relations.

Step by step.

  1. σcondition(R) keeps the rows that satisfy the condition, leaving the schema unchanged.
  2. πattr list(R) keeps the listed columns and discards the rest, leaving the rows filtered by duplicate elimination.
  3. ρ renames a relation or its attributes; ⋈ combines two relations on a condition.

Answer: Option (B) — π (projection).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) selects rows, not columns.
  • (C) only changes names.
  • (D) combines two relations.

Common misconception. σ and π are swapped. A quick check: σ for select rows (horizontal cut), π for project columns (vertical cut).

Takeaway. Picture the table: a horizontal cut removes rows (σ), a vertical cut removes columns (π).

DB-U1-Q19MSQmedium

Let R(A, B) and S(A, B) be union-compatible relations. Which of the following are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. R ∪ S contains every tuple that appears in R or in S or in both, with duplicates removed.
  2. R ∩ S can be written as R − (R − S).
  3. R − S = S − R for all R and S.
  4. R × S contains |R| + |S| tuples.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Set semantics. Union, intersection and difference behave as ordinary set operations once the schemas match; the Cartesian product multiplies cardinalities.

Step by step.

  1. (A) is the definition of set union; since the result is a relation, duplicates collapse.
  2. (B) R − S removes from R everything shared with S; removing those again from R leaves exactly the common part, so R − (R − S) = R ∩ S.
  3. (C) fails for any unequal pair, e.g. R = {t₁}, S = {t₂}.
  4. (D) is wrong: |R × S| = |R| · |S|.

Answer: Options (A) and (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) Difference is directional.
  • (D) The Cartesian product multiplies, it does not add.

Common misconception. Intersection is thought to need its own operator. It does not: ∩ is derivable from − (and from ⋈). Similarly ∪ is derivable from − and ×, which is why only six operators are primitive.

Takeaway. Memorise the derivations: R ∩ S = R − (R − S), and |R × S| = |R| × |S|.

DB-U1-Q20NAThard

Relation R(A, B, C) has 10 tuples and relation S(C, D) has 8 tuples, with C the only common attribute. The tuples of R use exactly 4 distinct values of C, and the tuples of S use exactly 5 distinct values of C. What is the maximum possible number of tuples in R ⋈ S?
(Enter an integer.)

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. The size of a natural join is Σc r_c · s_c over the common attribute, where r_c and s_c are the multiplicities of c in R and S. Under fixed totals the sum of products is largest when the two distributions are aligned and as concentrated as the distinct-value counts allow.

Step by step.

  1. Let r_c ≥ 1 for 4 values of C with Σ r_c = 10, and s_c ≥ 1 for 5 values of C with Σ s_c = 8. Then |R ⋈ S| = Σ_c r_c · s_c.
  2. Because there are 4 and 5 distinct values, at least 1 tuple must be spent on each of them. That leaves 10 − 4 = 6 extra R-tuples and 8 − 5 = 3 extra S-tuples to concentrate on one shared value c₀.
  3. Put all the extras on c₀: r = (7, 1, 1, 1) and s = (4, 1, 1, 1, 1).
  4. Matching the largest counts together gives 7 × 4 = 28 on c₀, and the three remaining shared values contribute 1 × 1 each: 28 + 3 = 31.
  5. No other distribution does better — by the rearrangement inequality the best alignment pairs the largest r_c with the largest s_c, and concentrating mass maximises the largest product.

Answer: 31.

Common misconception. Multiplying the totals, 10 × 8 = 80, or the distinct counts, 4 × 5 = 20. Both ignore the hard constraint that each of the distinct values must carry at least one tuple.

Takeaway. Subtract the compulsory one tuple per distinct value first; only the leftovers can be concentrated. Here 6 and 3 leftovers give 7 × 4 = 28 plus 3 forced unit products.

DB-U1-Q21MCQmedium

The tuple relational calculus query { t | t ∈ Student ∧ t[age] > 20 } is equivalent to

  1. π_age(σ_age>20(Student))
  2. σ_age>20(Student)
  3. σ_age>20(π_age(Student))
  4. Student ⋈ σ_age>20(Student)
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. In tuple calculus the free tuple variable ranges over all tuples of the relation named in the membership clause; a conjunctive condition on its attributes corresponds exactly to selection, and no projection is involved because t carries all attributes.

Step by step.

  1. t ∈ Student fixes the range of t to the tuples of Student.
  2. t[age] > 20 is the selection predicate.
  3. The result keeps whole tuples, so the algebra translation is σ_age>20(Student) with the same schema.
  4. Projection would be needed only if the query returned t[some attributes], not t itself.

Answer: Option (B) — σ_age>20(Student).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) changes the schema to a single column, which the query does not ask for.
  • (C) projects before selecting, which may drop attributes the predicate needs.
  • (D) joins Student with itself; the calculus query has one variable.

Common misconception. Students add a projection because the predicate mentions age. The predicate filters; it does not change the output schema. Only an explicit attribute list on t changes the schema.

Takeaway. Membership clause → which relation. Conditions on attributes → σ. t[list] on the left of the bar → π of that list.

DB-U1-Q22MCQeasy

In tuple relational calculus, the expression { t | ¬(t ∈ R) } is rejected as unsafe because

  1. it is syntactically invalid.
  2. it can denote an infinite relation, since t may take values outside the active domain.
  3. negation is not permitted in relational calculus.
  4. t is a free variable.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A calculus expression is safe when every value it can produce comes from the active domain — the set of values actually occurring in the database. { t | ¬(t ∈ R) } ranges over the infinite universe of all possible tuples, so it is not a finite relation and cannot be evaluated.

Step by step.

  1. The comprehension { t | ¬(t ∈ R) } admits every tuple not in R, over every possible attribute domain.
  2. That set is infinite (or at least unbounded), so it is not a relation in the database sense.
  3. Safety requires each answer tuple to be drawn from dom(R) extended by constants in the query.
  4. Negation itself is perfectly legal — only its unrestricted use is the problem.

Answer: Option (B) — it can denote an infinite relation.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) The syntax is well formed.
  • (C) Negation is allowed; unsafe negation is not.
  • (D) t is free by design — the free variable names the answer tuples.

Common misconception. Negation is blamed. The calculus allows ¬ freely; what it forbids is a query whose result is not confined to the active domain. { t | t ∈ R ∧ ¬P(t) } is safe, { t | ¬(t ∈ R) } is not.

Takeaway. Ask of every calculus query: which relation bounds the free variable? If nothing does, the query is unsafe.

DB-U1-Q23MSQmedium

Which of the following statements about relational calculus are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. Tuple relational calculus and domain relational calculus have the same expressive power.
  2. Every relational algebra expression can be written as a safe relational calculus expression.
  3. Relational calculus is procedural: it states how the answer is to be computed.
  4. The safety condition confines a query's values to the active domain of the database.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Relational algebra and the two calculi are all relationally complete: the algebra is procedural, the calculi are declarative, and all three express exactly the same class of queries once safety is imposed.

Step by step.

  1. (A) TRC and DRC are inter-translatable, so neither is more expressive — true.
  2. (B) Codd's theorem gives translations both ways between algebra and safe calculus — true.
  3. (C) Calculus says what the answer is (a set of tuples satisfying a formula); the algebra says how. So calculus is declarative, not procedural.
  4. (D) Safety restricts quantifiers and free variables to the active domain, which is what makes the expression finite — true.

Answer: Options (A), (B) and (D).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) reverses the definitions — calculus is declarative, not procedural.

Common misconception. Calculus is assumed to be the “stronger” formalism. In fact it has exactly the same power as the algebra; what it lacks is a fixed evaluation order, which is why SQL compilers translate to algebra.

Takeaway. Two things to keep straight: procedural = algebra, declarative = calculus, equal in power; and safety is the condition that makes calculus finite.

DB-U1-Q24NATmedium

Let R(A, B) = {(1, 5), (2, 5), (2, 7), (3, 5), (4, 9)}. The domain relational calculus query
{ ⟨a⟩ | ∃b (⟨a, b⟩ ∈ R ∧ b = 5) }
returns how many distinct values of a?
(Enter an integer.)

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A domain-calculus query projects the free variables of its formula. The existential quantifier on b makes b a bound (projected-away) variable, so the answer is the set of a-values that co-occur with b = 5.

Step by step.

  1. The formula requires a pair (a, b) in R with b = 5.
  2. Pairs with b = 5 are (1, 5), (2, 5) and (3, 5).
  3. Their a-values are 1, 2, 3 — three distinct values.
  4. (2, 7) and (4, 9) are excluded because their b is not 5.
  5. In algebra this is π_A(σ_B=5(R)), which has 3 tuples.

Answer: 3.

Common misconception. Counting tuples rather than distinct a-values, or being distracted by the repeated a = 2. The result of a calculus query is a set, so a = 2 contributes once.

Takeaway. Free variables = output columns. Here only a is free, so translate to π_A(σ_B=5(R)) and count.

DB-U1-Q25MCQhard

Which one of the following queries cannot be expressed in relational algebra, nor in safe relational calculus?

  1. Names of employees who earn more than their manager.
  2. Names of all employees who are, directly or indirectly, subordinates of a given employee.
  3. Names of employees who work on every project.
  4. Names of employees who work on no project.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Relational algebra and safe calculus are relationally complete but not Turing complete: they can express any finite first-order query over the stored relations, and nothing that needs an unbounded number of iteration steps. Transitive closure is the standard query outside that class.

Step by step.

  1. (A) is a self-join of Employee with itself on manager = eid plus a comparison — first-order, expressible.
  2. (C) is division: π_eid(Works) ÷ π_pid(Project) — expressible.
  3. (D) is a difference: π_eid(Employee) − π_eid(Works) — expressible.
  4. (B) asks for the transitive closure of the “manager of” relation. Its length is unbounded, so no fixed finite expression of the algebra or of first-order calculus can capture it.
  5. This is the classic reason SQL needed recursion (WITH RECURSIVE) to compute closure.

Answer: Option (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) needs one join and one comparison.
  • (C) is a textbook division query.
  • (D) is a set difference.

Common misconception. “Indirectly” is read as “one more join”. One extra join only reaches depth two; no finite number of joins reaches every depth, which is precisely why closure is not first-order expressible.

Faster method. Whenever a query says “directly or indirectly”, “any number of levels”, or “shortest path”, suspect transitive closure — outside relational algebra.

Takeaway. Distinguish the quantifier patterns. “For all projects” is a bounded ∀ and division handles it; “any number of steps” is an unbounded iteration and needs recursion.

What this test contains

25 questions on Unit 1 (ER Models & Relational Algebra), Set 2 of 2. All four subtopics are covered: ER diagrams, cardinality and weak entities; the relational model and its constraints; relational algebra (selection, projection, join, division); and tuple and domain relational calculus. Composition: 15 MCQ, 5 MSQ, 5 NAT; difficulty 10 easy, 10 medium, 5 hard; 60 minutes, 35 marks. MCQ carries 1 mark, MSQ and NAT carry 2 marks, there is no negative marking, and an MSQ scores only when the exact set of correct options is selected. This is a practice marking scheme, not the official GATE scheme.

Concept map used to build the questions

  • ER diagrams, cardinality and weak entities. Degree of a relationship set and why a ternary relationship is one diamond, not three; reading a cardinality ratio together with a participation constraint to bound a relationship's size — the ratio gives the maximum m · n, total participation gives the minimum max(m, n); the identifying relationship, the partial key and the owner-plus- partial key that identifies a weak entity; and the ER-to-relational recipe, where 1:N relationships are absorbed but multivalued attributes, weak entity sets, m:n and n-ary relationships each cost a relation.
  • Relational model and constraints. Superkey counting as 2^(n − k) for a single candidate key of size k, and the inclusion–exclusion correction when two keys exist; closure computation to enumerate candidate keys, including the case where a cyclic dependency leaves a whole family of two-attribute keys; Armstrong's three axioms and the invalid "converse of transitivity" that candidates routinely accept; entity integrity (no NULL in a primary key) against referential integrity (a foreign key may be NULL).
  • Relational algebra. Set semantics, so duplicates vanish; intersection derived as R − (R − S); natural join as a key lookup when the common attribute is a key on one side, so the row count cannot grow; theta joins on inequality, where no attribute is merged and the count is a filtered product; division as the "for all" operator, including the vacuous case R ÷ ∅ = πA(R); and the difference-of-difference pattern that turns an existential join into a universal statement.
  • Tuple and domain relational calculus. Free variables as the output columns; a shared bound variable as a self-join, so the answer counts ordered pairs; ∧, ∨ and ¬ translating directly from "and", "or", "not"; and safety as confinement to the active domain — a negation or a disjunction is safe only when a positive membership clause in the same conjunction still bounds the variable.
  • Connections. Closure and candidate-key counting return in Unit 3 (normalisation); the difference-over-join pattern underlies SQL's NOT EXISTS in Unit 2; join-size arithmetic and selectivity recur in Unit 5 (indexing and cost estimation).
