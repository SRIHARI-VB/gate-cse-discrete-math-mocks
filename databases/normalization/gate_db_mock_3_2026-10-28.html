DB-U1-Q01MCQeasy

In an entity–relationship diagram, a weak entity set is depicted by

  1. a single rectangle.
  2. a double rectangle.
  3. a single diamond.
  4. a double diamond.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. ER notation. A weak entity set has no key attribute of its own; its existence depends on an owner entity set, so it is drawn as a double rectangle. Its identifying relationship is drawn as a double diamond, its partial key is underlined with a dashed line, and its total participation in the identifying relationship is shown by a double line.

Step by step.

  1. A weak entity set cannot be identified by its own attributes, only by (owner key + partial key).
  2. The standard Chen notation distinguishes it with the double outline: double rectangle for the set, double diamond for the identifying relationship.
  3. Hence the correct symbol for the weak entity set itself is the double rectangle.

Answer: Option (B) — a double rectangle.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) A single rectangle denotes a strong (regular) entity set.
  • (C) A diamond denotes a relationship set, not an entity set.
  • (D) A double diamond denotes the identifying relationship of a weak entity set.

Common misconception. Confusing the double rectangle (weak entity set) with the double diamond (identifying relationship). They are different symbols for different things and a question may ask for either.

Faster method. If the option mentions a diamond at all, it is describing a relationship, not an entity set.

Takeaway. Fix the four ER symbols once: rectangle = entity set, double rectangle = weak entity set, diamond = relationship, double diamond = identifying relationship.

DB-U1-Q02MCQmedium

Entity set A has a many-to-one relationship R with entity set B. The participation of A in R is total and the participation of B in R is partial. Which one of the following is necessarily TRUE?

  1. Every entity of B is related to at least one entity of A.
  2. Every entity of A is related to exactly one entity of B.
  3. Every entity of B is related to exactly one entity of A.
  4. R must be merged into B because B's participation is partial.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Cardinality ratio plus participation constraint. “Many-to-one from A to B” means each entity of A is related to at most one entity of B, while one entity of B may be related to many entities of A. Total participation of A adds “at least one”, and the two bounds together give “exactly one”.

Step by step.

  1. Many-to-one A → B: an A-entity can be linked to at most one B-entity.
  2. Total participation of A: every A-entity is linked to at least one B-entity.
  3. At most one and at least one give exactly one B-entity for every A-entity.
  4. Partial participation of B only says some B-entities may have no A-entity at all; it constrains nothing about A.

Answer: Option (B) — every entity of A is related to exactly one entity of B.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) B's participation is partial, so a B-entity need not appear in R at all.
  • (C) Many-to-one allows many A-entities to share one B-entity, so B is not related to a unique A.
  • (D) A relationship is merged into an entity set only for a one-to-one or one-to-many case without loss, and partial participation of B does not force it.

Common misconception. Students read “partial participation of B” as if it weakened the constraint on A. Participation constrains only the entity set it is attached to.

Faster method. Total participation on the “many” side of a many-to-one turns “at most one” into “exactly one”.

Takeaway. Separate the two facts: the cardinality ratio bounds the count from above, participation bounds it from below. Combine them for the answer.

DB-U1-Q03MSQeasy

In a Chen-notation ER diagram, which of the following are drawn with a double outline or a double diamond? (Select all that apply.)

  1. A weak entity set.
  2. The identifying relationship of a weak entity set.
  3. A multivalued attribute.
  4. A derived attribute.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. ER notation distinguishes four special constructs. Double rectangle = weak entity set, double diamond = identifying relationship, double ellipse = multivalued attribute, dashed ellipse = derived attribute.

Step by step.

  1. (A) A weak entity set is drawn as a double rectangle — true.
  2. (B) The identifying relationship is drawn as a double diamond — true.
  3. (C) A multivalued attribute uses a double ellipse, not a double outline on a set or a relationship.
  4. (D) A derived attribute uses a dashed ellipse.

Answer: Options (A) and (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) uses a double ellipse, which is a different shape.
  • (D) uses a dashed ellipse.

Common misconception. The double ellipse for a multivalued attribute is confused with the double rectangle for a weak entity set. Both are “double”, but the shape differs and so does the meaning.

Takeaway. Remember the pairing shape-to-meaning: double rectangle / double diamond belong together as the weak-entity pair; double ellipse is an attribute-level symbol.

DB-U1-Q04NATmedium

Entity set E owns weak entity set W through an identifying relationship R. E has 4 entities, R permits at most 3 W-entities per E-entity, and every E-entity participates totally in R. What is the maximum possible number of entities in W?
(Enter an integer.)

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A weak entity set's cardinality is bounded by the owner's cardinality times the per-owner limit. Total participation of the owner gives a lower bound, the per-owner limit gives the upper bound.

Step by step.

  1. Every E-entity must own at least one W-entity (total participation), so |W| ≥ 4.
  2. Each E-entity may own at most 3 W-entities, so |W| ≤ 4 × 3 = 12.
  3. The upper bound 12 is attained when all 4 E-entities own the full 3 W-entities each.
  4. So the maximum is 12.

Answer: 12.

Common misconception. Adding the two numbers instead of multiplying, or forgetting that a weak entity is owned by exactly one owner — which is what makes the count a product rather than a sum.

Takeaway. For an identifying relationship the owner side is one-to-many, so multiply the owner count by the per-owner limit; the owner's participation decides the minimum, the limit decides the maximum.

DB-U1-Q05MCQhard

In ER modelling, aggregation is used to

  1. combine several attributes of one entity set into a single composite attribute.
  2. treat a relationship set together with its participating entity sets as one higher-level entity set that can then take part in another relationship.
  3. replace a weak entity set by a strong entity set.
  4. convert a ternary relationship into three binary relationships.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Aggregation is the ER construct that removes a limitation of plain ER diagrams: a relationship set cannot participate in another relationship. Aggregating a relationship plus its entity sets yields a single abstract entity that can.

Step by step.

  1. Plain ER forbids a relationship set from being an endpoint of another relationship.
  2. Example: a project is done by employees using machines. “Uses” relates the (employee, project) pair to a machine, not an employee to a machine directly.
  3. Aggregation groups Employee–Works_On–Project into one entity set and lets that set participate in Uses.
  4. So aggregation abstracts a relationship together with its participants into a higher-level entity set.

Answer: Option (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) describes a composite attribute, drawn as an ellipse with sub-ellipses.
  • (C) is not an ER operation at all; a weak entity set stays weak unless the schema is redesigned.
  • (D) is a modelling decision about arity, not aggregation.

Common misconception. Aggregation is confused with generalization (which factors common attributes of entity sets into a superclass) and with the composite-attribute notation. All three “combine” things, but at different levels.

Faster method. If the sentence mentions “a relationship participating in another relationship”, the answer is aggregation.

Takeaway. Aggregation = relationship-as-entity. Generalization = entity-as-subclass. Composite attribute = attribute-of-attribute.

DB-U1-Q06MCQeasy

A multivalued attribute in an ER diagram is represented by

  1. a single ellipse.
  2. a double ellipse.
  3. a dashed ellipse.
  4. a rectangle.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Attribute notation in Chen's model. A multivalued attribute can hold a set of values for one entity, and is drawn as a double ellipse. A derived attribute is drawn with a dashed ellipse; a key attribute has its name underlined.

Step by step.

  1. Multivalued means several values per entity, e.g. phone numbers of a person.
  2. The extra value needs an extra outline, so the symbol is the double ellipse.
  3. A derived attribute (computed from others, e.g. age from date of birth) uses a dashed ellipse instead.

Answer: Option (B) — a double ellipse.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) A single ellipse is a simple, single-valued attribute.
  • (C) A dashed ellipse is a derived attribute.
  • (D) A rectangle is an entity set.

Common misconception. The double ellipse is confused with the double rectangle of a weak entity set. Note that the doubling pattern is applied to the base shape: rectangle for sets, ellipse for attributes.

Takeaway. Learn the four attribute shapes together: single ellipse (simple), double ellipse (multivalued), dashed ellipse (derived), underlined name (key).

DB-U1-Q26MCQeasy

In Chen notation, a relationship set of degree three is drawn as

  1. a single diamond joined by three lines, one to each of the three participating entity sets.
  2. three diamonds, each joined to two entity sets.
  3. a double diamond joined to three entity sets.
  4. a rectangle joined to three diamonds.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. The degree of a relationship set is the number of entity sets that take part in it. Chen notation draws one relationship as one diamond, with one line per participating entity set — so degree three is one diamond with three lines.

Step by step.

  1. Count the entity sets that take part: three.
  2. Draw one diamond — the relationship itself is a single object, not one object per pair.
  3. Draw one connecting line from the diamond to each of the three entity sets.
  4. Label each line with that entity set's cardinality contribution if the diagram shows it.

Answer: Option (A) — one diamond with three lines.

Common misconception. Reading a ternary relationship as three binary diamonds. Three binary relationships record three independent pairwise facts and cannot express the single triple fact that a ternary relationship records.

DB-U1-Q27MCQmedium

Entity sets A (m entities) and B (n entities), with m, n ≥ 1, take part in a many-to-many relationship R, and both participations are total. The number of tuples in the relation that represents R lies

  1. exactly at m · n.
  2. between max(m, n) and m · n, inclusive.
  3. between m + n and m · n, inclusive.
  4. exactly at m + n.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Two independent constraints bound a many-to-many relationship: the ratio gives the upper bound and total participation gives the lower bound.

Step by step.

  1. Upper bound: each of the m A-entities may pair with each of the n B-entities, so at most m · n distinct pairs.
  2. Lower bound from A's side: total participation means every A-entity appears in at least one pair, so at least m pairs.
  3. Lower bound from B's side: every B-entity appears in at least one pair, so at least n pairs.
  4. Pairs are distinct tuples, so the count is at least the larger of the two bounds, i.e. at least max(m, n). Both bounds are attainable.
  5. Hence max(m, n) ≤ |R| ≤ m · n.

Answer: Option (B).

Common misconception. Adding the cardinalities (m + n) or assuming the maximum is forced. The maximum m · n needs every A-entity to pair with every B-entity, which the m:n ratio permits but does not require.

DB-U1-Q28MSQmedium

W is a weak entity set owned by entity set E through the identifying relationship R. Which of the following statements are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. W has a partial key (discriminator) that distinguishes W-entities owned by the same E-entity.
  2. The participation of W in R is total.
  3. The primary key of the relation built from W is the primary key of E together with W's partial key.
  4. A W-entity may exist without being related to any E-entity.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A weak entity set has no key of its own. It is identified by its owner, and its existence is dependent on that owner — existence dependency is exactly what 'weak' means.

Step by step.

  1. Because W has no key of its own, it carries a partial key (drawn with a dashed underline) that only has to be unique within one owner's set of W-entities — so (A) is true.
  2. A weak entity cannot be identified without its owner, so it must always take part in the identifying relationship — so (B) is true.
  3. The identifying relationship is one-to-many from E to W, so the owner's key together with the partial key is unique over all of W — so (C) is true.
  4. (D) contradicts existence dependency: an owner-less weak entity has no identity at all — so (D) is false.

Answer: Options (A), (B) and (C).

Common misconception. Thinking the partial key alone identifies a weak entity. It does not: two different owners may each own a W-entity with the same partial-key value, which is why the owner's key must be prefixed.

DB-U1-Q29NAThard

An ER schema contains entity sets A, B, C, D; a weak entity set W owned by A; a multivalued attribute phone on B; a 1:N relationship from A to C; a many-to-many relationship between B and D; and a ternary relationship among A, B and C.
Applying the standard ER-to-relational mapping — one relation per strong or weak entity set, a separate relation per multivalued attribute, a 1:N relationship absorbed into the relation on its many side, and a separate relation per m:n or n-ary relationship — how many relations does the schema produce?

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. The ER-to-relational mapping is a fixed recipe; the question is which constructs cost a relation and which are absorbed into an existing one.

Step by step.

  1. Strong entity sets A, B, C, D → 4 relations.
  2. Weak entity set W → 1 more relation (it becomes a relation whose key is A's key plus W's partial key) → 5.
  3. Multivalued attribute phone → 1 more relation (owner key + phone value) → 6.
  4. 1:N relationship A → C → 0 new relations; it is absorbed as a foreign key in C → still 6.
  5. m:n relationship B–D → 1 more relation → 7.
  6. Ternary relationship among A, B, C → 1 more relation → 8.

Answer: 8

Common misconception. Charging a relation for the 1:N relationship, or forgetting that a weak entity set still gets its own relation. The 1:N side is the free one; the weak entity set is not.

Takeaway. Only relationships whose cardinality cannot be carried by a foreign key — m:n and n-ary — need a relation of their own.

DB-U1-Q30MCQhard

Which one of the following statements about a ternary relationship R among entity sets A, B and C is TRUE?

  1. R is always equivalent to three binary relationships among A, B and C.
  2. R can record a combination of an A-entity, a B-entity and a C-entity that no collection of binary relationships can record.
  3. R must be many-to-many in all three directions.
  4. R requires every entity to take part exactly once.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A relationship of degree three is an association among three entities simultaneously, not three associations among pairs.

Step by step.

  1. A ternary relationship instance is a triple (a, b, c).
  2. Three binary relationships would instead store (a, b), (b, c) and (a, c) separately. From those three pairs you cannot tell which triples actually occurred: the pairs (a1, b1), (b1, c1), (a1, c1) are consistent with both (a1, b1, c1) and with (a1, b1, c2), (a1, b2, c1), …
  3. So the ternary relationship carries information the binary decomposition loses — (B) is true and (A) is false.
  4. Nothing forces the ratio to be m:n in every direction, and nothing forces single participation — (C) and (D) are false.

Answer: Option (B).

Common misconception. Assuming a ternary relationship is just 'three binary ones glued together'. It is not, and that is precisely why the ER-to-relational mapping gives an n-ary relationship its own relation.

DB-U1-Q31MCQeasy

Entity set A has a one-to-many relationship R with entity set B — that is, one A-entity is related to many B-entities. Which statement is TRUE?

  1. Each B-entity is related to at most one A-entity.
  2. Each A-entity is related to at most one B-entity.
  3. Each A-entity must be related to at least one B-entity.
  4. Each B-entity must be related to at least one A-entity.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A cardinality ratio constrains how many entities on one side may be associated with an entity on the other. It says nothing about whether an entity must be associated at all — that is participation, a separate constraint.

Step by step.

  1. 'One A to many B' means the function runs from B to A: each B-entity picks at most one A-entity.
  2. So (A) is true.
  3. The same ratio allows an A-entity to have any number of B-entities, including zero, so (B) is false.
  4. (C) and (D) assert total participation, which the question never states. Both are false.

Answer: Option (A).

Common misconception. Reading 'one-to-many from A to B' as 'one B per A'. The 'one' side is the side that is unique in the other direction: one A per B.

DB-U1-Q07MCQeasy

Relation R(A, B, C) has primary key (A, B) and a foreign key C that references S(C). Which one of the following is TRUE?

  1. A and B cannot be NULL, but C may be NULL.
  2. C cannot be NULL, but A may be NULL.
  3. Any of A, B, C may be NULL.
  4. A alone must be unique.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Entity integrity says no attribute of a primary key may be NULL; referential integrity says a foreign key must either be NULL or match an existing referenced value. The two rules apply to different attributes here, so both can hold at once.

Step by step.

  1. Entity integrity: every attribute of the primary key (A, B) is NOT NULL. So A and B cannot be NULL.
  2. Referential integrity constrains C: each C-value must be NULL or equal some S.C value.
  3. Nothing forbids C from being NULL, because C is not part of R's primary key.
  4. Hence (A, B) are mandatory, C is optional.

Answer: Option (A).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (B) reverses the two rules.
  • (C) contradicts entity integrity.
  • (D) A is only part of the key; A alone may repeat.

Common misconception. Students assume a foreign key can never be NULL. It can, provided the FK attributes are not part of the primary key — that is exactly how “optional” relationships are modelled.

Takeaway. Check whether the FK overlaps the primary key. No overlap → NULL is allowed. Overlap → entity integrity forbids NULL.

DB-U1-Q08MCQmedium

Let R(A, B, C, D) satisfy the functional dependencies {AB → C, C → D, D → A}. How many candidate keys does R have?

  1. 1
  2. 2
  3. 3
  4. 4
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A candidate key is a minimal set of attributes whose closure is the whole relation. Compute closures of each attribute alone, then of the promising pairs.

Step by step.

  1. A⁺ = A; B⁺ = B; C⁺ = C D A = ACD (using C → D and D → A); D⁺ = D A = AD. None is the whole relation.
  2. AB⁺ = A B C D = ABCD. AB is a key, and neither A nor B alone is, so AB is a candidate key.
  3. BC⁺ = B C D A = ABCD. BC is minimal (B⁺, C⁺ both fail), so BC is a candidate key.
  4. BD⁺ = B D A = ABD, and AB → C applies, so BD⁺ = ABCD. BD is minimal, so BD is a candidate key.
  5. AC⁺ = ACD, AD⁺ = AD, CD⁺ = ACD — none is the whole relation. The answer is 3.

Answer: Option (C) — 3 candidate keys (AB, BC, BD).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) misses BC and BD.
  • (B) misses BD.
  • (D) over-counts; AC, AD and CD are not keys.

Common misconception. Stopping at AB, or counting AB and BA as different keys. Also easy to miss BD, which needs the two-step closure D → A followed by AB → C.

Faster method. Here C and D never appear in a closure that contains B, so B is in every key. That forces you to test AB, BC and BD — exactly three.

Takeaway. Write every single-attribute closure first. Any attribute absent from all of them must appear in every candidate key; then test the minimal pairs containing it.

DB-U1-Q09MSQmedium

Which of the following statements about keys of a relation are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. Every relation has at least one candidate key.
  2. A superkey that has no proper subset which is also a superkey is a candidate key.
  3. A relation can have at most one candidate key.
  4. A primary key attribute may contain NULL values.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Keys are defined through functional determination on the whole set of attributes. The set of all attributes of a relation is always a superkey, so minimal superkeys always exist.

Step by step.

  1. The full attribute set functionally determines itself, so it is a superkey; shrinking it until it is minimal yields a candidate key. Hence at least one candidate key always exists.
  2. A candidate key is precisely a minimal superkey — that is the definition given in (B).
  3. A relation may have several candidate keys; one of them is chosen as the primary key.
  4. Entity integrity forbids NULL in any primary key attribute.

Answer: Options (A) and (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (C) is false — a relation may have several candidate keys, and exactly one is picked as primary.
  • (D) is false — entity integrity requires primary key attributes to be NOT NULL.

Common misconception. The word “candidate” is read as “optional”, suggesting there might be none. Every relation has at least one; what varies is how many.

Takeaway. Two facts to keep: the full attribute set is always a superkey (so keys exist), and minimality is what separates candidate keys from superkeys.

Which statement about ON and WHERE in an inner join is TRUE?

  1. ON carries only equality conditions; an inequality belongs in WHERE.
  2. ON is evaluated after WHERE.
  3. They give different results whenever a joined column contains a NULL.
  4. For an inner join, moving a predicate from ON to WHERE gives the same result.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. For an inner join the two clauses are interchangeable, because both simply discard rows that fail the predicate. The distinction only becomes visible with an outer join, where ON decides which pairs match but WHERE runs after the NULL-extension and can remove the preserved rows.

Step by step.

  1. An inner join keeps exactly the pairs for which the ON predicate is TRUE.
  2. A WHERE predicate is also applied to those same pairs, before the result is produced.
  3. Conjunction is commutative, so applying either predicate first leaves the same set of pairs.
  4. Hence the two placements are equivalent for an inner join — and only for an inner join.

Answer: Option (D).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) is false: ON accepts any boolean expression, including inequalities and conjunctions.
  • (B) reverses the order; ON determines the pairs, WHERE filters afterwards.
  • (C) is false for inner joins; NULL behaviour is the same under either placement.

Common misconception. Generalising the outer-join rule to inner joins. The placement matters only when there are NULL-extended rows to lose.

Takeaway. Inner join: ON and WHERE are equivalent. Outer join: ON matches, WHERE prunes.

Detailed solution

Concept. A join with no effective condition is a Cartesian product, whether it is written CROSS JOIN or INNER JOIN ... ON TRUE. Which statement about an SQL view is TRUE?

  1. A view always stores a private copy of the rows it returns, refreshed on every insert into the base table.
  2. A view is a named query: it stores no rows of its own (unless it is a materialised view) and its rows are recomputed from the base tables when the view is queried.
  3. A view must be defined over exactly one base table.
  4. Dropping a view deletes the rows of the underlying table.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. In standard SQL a view is a derived table: the catalogue stores only the defining query. Every reference to the view expands to that query, so the view always reflects the current base tables. A materialised view is the exception — it does keep a stored copy and must be refreshed — but that is an optimisation feature, not what a plain CREATE VIEW gives you.

Step by step.

  1. The definition is stored as text; no tuples are copied.
  2. Querying the view runs (or rewrites) the defining query against the base tables.
  3. Therefore the view is always consistent with the base data, with no refresh step.

Answer: Option (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) describes a materialised view, and even then the refresh is explicit or scheduled, not automatic on every insert.
  • (C) is false: a view may join any number of tables, as the aggregate view in this unit does.
  • (D) is false: DROP VIEW removes only the definition; the base tables and their rows are untouched.

Common misconception. Believing a view is a snapshot. Because it is recomputed, a view over student immediately shows a newly inserted student — there is nothing to refresh.

Takeaway. A view is stored logic, not stored data.

DB-U2-Q22MCQeasy

Which statement about an SQL trigger is TRUE?

  1. A trigger fires only for SELECT statements.
  2. A trigger must be called explicitly, for example with EXECUTE TRIGGER, after the statement it is meant to guard.
  3. A trigger fires automatically when its specified event (such as an INSERT, UPDATE or DELETE) occurs; it cannot be invoked directly by a statement.
  4. A table that has triggers may not also have constraints.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A trigger is an event–condition–action rule attached to a table or view. The database fires it as a side effect of the triggering statement; there is no SQL statement that calls a trigger by name. Triggers are declared BEFORE or AFTER the event, and FOR EACH ROW or FOR EACH STATEMENT.

Step by step.

  1. The event is a DML change (in standard SQL), optionally restricted with WHEN to specific columns.
  2. The action is a block of SQL executed by the engine when the event occurs.
  3. No user statement names the trigger, so it cannot be called directly.

Answer: Option (C).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (B) there is no such statement in standard SQL.
  • (A) triggers are defined on data-changing events; some systems add INSTEAD OF triggers on views, but a plain SELECT does not fire one.
  • (D) triggers and constraints are independent mechanisms and routinely coexist; constraints are usually preferred for declarative rules because they are cheaper.

Common misconception. Thinking a trigger replaces a foreign key. Triggers can enforce rules that constraints cannot express, but they are procedural, harder to reason about, and a well-modelled schema uses constraints first.

Takeaway. Triggers are fired by the engine, never called by the user.
