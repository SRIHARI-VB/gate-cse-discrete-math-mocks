Concept. Under FCFS the processes run in arrival order without interruption. Waiting time is turnaround time minus burst time, and turnaround time is completion time minus arrival time.

  1. P1 runs from 0 to 3: completion 3, waiting time 3 − 0 − 3 = 0.

OS-U1-Q20MCQeasy

The convoy effect — several short CPU-bound processes queued behind one long CPU-bound process, producing a poor average waiting time — is most closely associated with which algorithm?

  1. Round robin
  2. First come first served
  3. Shortest remaining time first
  4. Multilevel feedback queue
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. The convoy effect is a direct consequence of serving processes in arrival order regardless of length, which is exactly what FCFS does.

Step by step.

  1. Under FCFS one long job at the head of the queue makes every short job behind it wait.
  2. The short jobs also hold up the I/O device they use only briefly, so device utilisation suffers too.
  3. SRTF would move the short jobs ahead, and round robin would interleave them, so neither produces the convoy.
  4. Hence the convoy effect belongs to FCFS.

Answer: Option (B) — first come first served.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) Round robin interleaves jobs and bounds the wait by the quantum.
  • (C) SRTF always runs the shortest remaining job, which dissolves the convoy.
  • (D) Multilevel feedback queues promote short jobs, which also dissolves it.

Common misconception. The convoy effect is sometimes attached to SJF because SJF is 'the fix'. SJF avoids the convoy; FCFS causes it.

Takeaway. Convoy effect = FCFS + one long job at the head. The standard remedies are SJF, SRTF and round robin.

OS-U1-Q21MCQmedium

Three processes arrive at time <span class="mm">t = 0</span> with CPU bursts of 12 ms, 18 ms and 30 ms. The scheduler knows the burst lengths in advance. What is the minimum achievable average waiting time for these three processes under a <b>non-preemptive</b> scheduler?

  1. 12 ms
  2. 14 ms
  3. 16 ms
  4. 18 ms
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. For a fixed set of processes with no arrival spread, the non-preemptive algorithm that minimises average waiting time is shortest job first: run the shortest burst first and the longest last.

Step by step.

  1. Order the bursts: 12, 18, 30.
  2. SJF runs them in that order. Waiting times: first job 0, second 12, third 12 + 18 = 30.
  3. Average = (0 + 12 + 30) / 3 = 42 / 3 = 14 ms.
  4. Any other order increases the sum, so 14 ms is the minimum.

Answer: Option (B) — 14 ms.

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) 12 ms is the waiting time of the second job alone.
  • (C) and (D) come from averaging turnaround times, or from running the jobs in arrival order and dividing incorrectly.

Common misconception. Averaging turnaround times instead of waiting times. With all arrivals at t = 0 the turnaround times are 12, 30, 60, whose average is 34 ms — a different quantity.

Takeaway. All arriving together + non-preemptive + 'minimum average waiting time' means sort the bursts ascending and accumulate: 0, b₁, b₁ + b₂.

OS-U1-Q23MSQhard

Which of the following statements about CPU scheduling algorithms are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. SRTF can starve a long process indefinitely if short processes keep arriving.
  2. Increasing the time quantum of round robin increases the number of context switches.
  3. Among non-preemptive algorithms, SJF minimises the average waiting time.
  4. FCFS always gives the minimum average turnaround time for a given set of processes.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Starvation, quantum size and SJF optimality are the three classic scheduling properties; FCFS optimality is the classic false claim.

Step by step.

  1. (A) True: SRTF always picks the shortest remaining time, so a long job loses to every short arrival and may never run.
  2. (B) False: a larger quantum means fewer expiries, hence fewer context switches — with a quantum larger than every burst, round robin degenerates into FCFS.
  3. (C) True: SJF is provably optimal for average waiting time among non-preemptive algorithms, by an exchange argument on adjacent jobs.
  4. (D) False: FCFS can be arbitrarily bad — the convoy effect — so it cannot be optimal.

Answer: Options (A) and (C).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (B) reverses the relationship: the number of switches falls as the quantum grows.
  • (D) is false; SJF, not FCFS, is the optimal non-preemptive rule for waiting time.

Common misconception. Option (B) is chosen because a smaller quantum is associated with 'more responsive', which students turn into 'more switches when the quantum grows'. It is the other way round.

Takeaway. Quantum up → switches down, response worse. Quantum down → switches up, overhead up. SJF is optimal for waiting time; nothing is optimal for turnaround in general except in special cases.

Detailed solution

Concept. Under SRTF the process with the smallest remaining burst always holds the CPU, and the comparison is repeated at every arrival. Waiting time = completion − arrival − burst.

Step by step.

  1. t = 0: P1 runs. At t = 2 its remaining time is 5.
  2. t = 2: P2 arrives with burst 4 < 5, so P2 preempts P1.
  3. t = 4: P3 arrives with burst 1 < P2's remaining 2, so P3 preempts P2.
  4. t = 5: P3 completes. Remaining: P2 = 2, P1 = 5, P4 = 4 (P4 arrived at t = 5). P2 runs.
  5. t = 7: P2 completes. Remaining: P4 = 4, P1 = 5. P4 runs.
  6. t = 11: P4 completes. P1 runs its remaining 5 ms and completes at t = 16.
  7. Completion times: P1 = 16, P2 = 7, P3 = 5, P4 = 11.
  8. Waiting times: P1 = 16 − 0 − 7 = 9; P2 = 7 − 2 − 4 = 1; P3 = 5 − 4 − 1 = 0; P4 = 11 − 5 − 4 = 2.
  9. Average = (9 + 1 + 0 + 2) / 4 = 12 / 4 = 3 ms.

A counting semaphore used to control access to 5 identical printers is initialised to 5. At some instant its value is −2, under the convention in which a negative value counts the blocked processes. How many processes are blocked on the semaphore at that instant?

Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. Under the negative-value convention the magnitude of the value is exactly the length of the wait queue.

Step by step.

  1. The semaphore started at 5, one permit per printer.
  2. Each blocked process contributes 1 to the negative part of the value.
  3. The value is −2, so |−2| = 2 processes are waiting for a printer.

Answer: 2.

Common misconception. Reporting 5 − 2 = 3 by treating the value as a count of free printers; a negative value counts waiters, not permits.

OS-U2B-Q17MCQmedium

Three processes share a binary semaphore <code class="mm">mutex</code> initialised to 1. Process A is inside its critical section, having executed <code class="mm">P(mutex)</code>. Processes B and C now both execute <code class="mm">P(mutex)</code> and block. What is the value of <code class="mm">mutex</code>, and how many processes are blocked?

  1. 0, with one process blocked.
  2. −2, with two processes blocked.
  3. 1, with no process blocked.
  4. −1, with two processes blocked.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. The same decrement-then-test rule applies to a binary semaphore; only its permitted values on the way up are bounded by its use as a mutex.

Step by step.

  1. Initial value 1. A executes P: 1 → 0, and A continues because the value was positive before the decrement.
  2. B executes P: 0 → −1, and B blocks.
  3. C executes P: −1 → −2, and C blocks.
  4. After B the value is −1 and after C it is −2; the magnitude 2 is exactly the number of waiters.

Answer: Option (D).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) stops counting after the first waiter.
  • (B) double-counts by decrementing once for the holder as well.
  • (C) ignores the two blocked calls entirely.

Common misconception. A binary semaphore's value is binary only while it is non-negative. Once waiters queue up, the value goes negative and counts them.

OS-U2B-Q18MSQmedium

Which of the following statements about semaphore values and semaphore operations are TRUE? (Select all that apply.)

  1. Blocking inside <code class="mm">P</code> rather than spinning avoids wasting a processor while the process waits.
  2. A counting semaphore initialised to 0, with no blocked process, means that no unit of the resource is currently available.
  3. If <code class="mm">P</code> were not executed atomically, two processes could both observe a positive value and both decrement it, so mutual exclusion would fail.
  4. A counting semaphore initialised to 5 gives mutual exclusion for a critical section in exactly the same way as one initialised to 1.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. A semaphore is a counter plus an atomic decrement-or-block and an atomic increment-or-wake.

Step by step.

  1. The wait queue is the point of the construct: the waiting process is descheduled instead of burning cycles.
  2. The initial value is the number of permits, so 0 permits means every unit is in use.
  3. The atomicity of P is what prevents the lost-update race between two decrements.
  4. A semaphore initialised to 5 admits five processes into the section at once, which is the opposite of mutual exclusion.

Answer: Options (A), (B) and (C).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (D) is the statement to reject: the initial value must be 1 for the semaphore to act as a mutex.

Common misconception. The initial value is not decoration — it is the admission count. Only 1 gives mutual exclusion.

OS-U2B-Q19MCQhard

One implementation of <code class="mm">P</code> and <code class="mm">V</code> disables interrupts while the semaphore value is tested and updated, which makes the pair atomic on a uniprocessor. On a multiprocessor the same implementation is unsafe because:

  1. disabling interrupts is illegal while a process is in user mode, so <code class="mm">P</code> and <code class="mm">V</code> could never be called.
  2. the interrupt mask is per processor, so another processor can execute <code class="mm">P</code> on the same semaphore at the same instant and the update is no longer atomic.
  3. disabling interrupts makes <code class="mm">P</code> and <code class="mm">V</code> take unbounded time, which breaks bounded waiting.
  4. the semaphore value is held in a register rather than in memory, so the mask does not protect it.
Show answer & solution

Detailed solution

Concept. An interrupt mask stops local preemption; it says nothing about another processor running in parallel.

Step by step.

  1. On a uniprocessor, disabling interrupts prevents any other process from being scheduled in the middle of the update, which is enough for atomicity.
  2. On a multiprocessor, the other processors keep running with their own masks, so two P operations can still interleave.
  3. The correct primitive is an atomic instruction (test-and-set, swap, compare-and-swap) on the semaphore's word, or a spin lock around the update.

Answer: Option (B).

Why the other options are wrong.

  • (A) is a privilege question, not the atomicity question being asked.
  • (C) is a timing concern, not a correctness one.
  • (D) is false: the semaphore is a shared memory word.

Common misconception. Interrupt masking is the standard uniprocessor answer and the standard multiprocessor mistake. The mask is local to one processor.
