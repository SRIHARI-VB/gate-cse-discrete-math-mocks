What this test contains

25 questions on Unit 1 (Process Management & CPU Scheduling), Set 1 of 2. All four subtopics are covered: process states and the PCB; user-level against kernel-level threads; preemptive against non-preemptive scheduling; and the FCFS, SJF, SRTF, round-robin and priority algorithms. Composition: 15 MCQ, 5 MSQ, 5 NAT; difficulty 10 easy, 10 medium, 5 hard; 60 minutes, 35 marks. MCQ carries 1 mark, MSQ and NAT carry 2 marks, there is no negative marking, and an MSQ scores only when the exact set of correct options is selected. This is a practice marking scheme, not the official GATE scheme.

Concept map used to build the questions

  • Process states and the PCB. The five-state diagram and which edges actually exist — Running leaves only to Waiting or Terminated when nothing is preemptive; the seven-state model with the two suspended states, where leaving a suspended state means being swapped back into main memory; what a PCB holds (identity, state, saved context, scheduling, memory and I/O data) and what it does not (the code itself); a blocking call as Running → Waiting → Ready; context switching as pure overhead, distinct from a mode switch; and fork() doubling the process count on every call.
  • Threads, user-level and kernel-level. What is shared (code, globals, heap, open files) and what is private (stack, registers, program counter); the three threading models, and why many-to-one is confined to one core; the blocking behaviour that separates the two kinds of thread; and the non-preemptive user-level scheduler that starves its own siblings when one thread never yields.
  • Preemptive and non-preemptive scheduling. What can displace a running process — quantum expiry, a higher-priority arrival, a shorter remaining time — and what cannot, such as another process merely becoming ready; the voluntary exits that non-preemptive scheduling relies on; and a second-by-second trace of preemptive priority scheduling that counts preemptions rather than dispatches.
  • FCFS, SJF, SRTF, round robin and priority. Completion, turnaround and waiting time with TAT = completion − arrival and WT = TAT − burst; the convoy effect under FCFS; SJF optimality for average waiting time among non-preemptive algorithms, and the accumulation 0, b₁, b₁ + b₂ that proves it; a full round-robin trace with a FIFO ready queue; and the quantum trade-off — a larger quantum means fewer context switches but worse response.
  • Connections. Context-switch cost returns in Unit 2 (synchronisation) and Unit 4 (paging and TLB cost); the scheduling arithmetic feeds the effective-access-time calculations of Unit 5; and thread models reappear in Unit 2 when semaphores must protect shared state between threads of one process.
